Meetings and interviews are full of information that nobody manages to write down. Transcribing them changes that: you get a searchable record, accurate action items, and verbatim quotes you can actually verify. For conversations in Indian languages, transcription also solves a practical problem, because most meeting notes are still written in English even when the discussion happens in Hindi, Tamil, Kannada or Telugu. This guide walks through the full workflow: record, transcribe, edit, and share, with practical tips on language selection and what speech to text can and cannot do with multiple speakers.

## Why transcribe meetings at all

A transcript is more than a backup. It makes a two-hour meeting searchable in seconds, settles disagreements about what was decided, captures the exact wording of a customer complaint or a source's quote, and turns spoken discussions into shareable documents for people who were not there. In interviews, a transcript is the raw material of the article: quotes, context and sequence all preserved exactly. The effort is small compared to the payoff, especially now that Indian-language transcription is practical.

## Step 1: Record clean audio

Transcription quality starts with the recording, not the model. A transcript can only be as good as the audio it comes from. A few habits make the difference between a clean transcript and a frustrating one:

- **Place the recorder close to the speakers.** A phone in the middle of the table beats a phone in someone's pocket. For interviews, put the recorder near the interviewee.
- **Keep the room quiet.** Fan noise, traffic, and side conversations all degrade accuracy. Close the door and mute notifications.
- **Avoid overlapping speech.** Speech to text handles one voice at a time; when two people talk at once, both get garbled. In meetings, note when people interrupt and ask for turns if it matters.
- **Check the recording early.** Ten seconds in, play back a snippet to confirm the audio is actually being captured and is audible.
- **Get consent.** Informing participants that the meeting is being recorded and transcribed is both professional courtesy and, in many contexts, a legal requirement. Say it clearly at the start.

## Step 2: Transcribe in the right language

Language selection is the single most important setting for an accurate transcript. Speech to text works in one language at a time, so decide on the dominant language of the conversation and select it in the app. A meeting that is mostly Hindi with occasional English words should be transcribed in Hindi; a Telugu interview with the occasional English technical term should be transcribed in Telugu. Mixed-code speech produces mixed results, so match the setting to the majority of the audio.

## Multi-speaker audio: what speech to text can and cannot do

Set the right expectation: speech to text transcribes, it does not attribute. It produces a single running transcript of everything that was said, without speaker labels. It will not tell you that Priya made the point about the budget and that Ravi disagreed. That is a limitation of the technology itself, not of any particular app. If you need attribution, add names while editing, in the same way you would clean up any transcript. If you only need the content, the running transcript is enough, and the desktop app's recording history with search makes it easy to find specific passages later.

## Step 3: Edit before you share

No speech to text system is error-free, and meetings add difficulty: names, product terms, numbers and accent variation all create mistakes. Plan to spend a few minutes per hour of audio cleaning up the transcript:

- **Fix names and jargon.** People, companies and technical terms are the most common errors, and the most damaging ones in a shared document.
- **Verify numbers and dates.** Numerals are easy to mishear; check every figure against the recording or your memory.
- **Remove filler if it matters.** If the transcript is going to colleagues, filler words add noise. Veena's desktop app can optionally remove filler words, and custom-word corrections can teach it the names and terms your team uses.
- **Keep it verbatim or clean it up.** Decide up front whether you want a word-for-word record or a cleaned-up summary, and edit accordingly. For interviews, keeping the quote verbatim matters; for meeting minutes, a cleaned version is usually more useful.

## Step 4: Share and keep

Once the transcript is clean, put it where the people who need it can find it. Copy it into an email, paste it into your notes app, or share it directly from the mobile app. On desktop, the recording history keeps past sessions searchable, and retention settings let you control how long recordings and transcripts are kept. Because transcription is on-device, the transcript is yours: no cloud service stores a copy of your meeting, and your audio never leaves your device. ## The bottom line

Transcribing meetings and interviews in Indian languages is a four-step workflow: record clean audio, transcribe in the correct language, edit the names and numbers, and share the result. Keep the microphone close to the speakers, expect to add speaker labels yourself, and you will end up with a searchable, shareable record that hand-written notes can never match.
