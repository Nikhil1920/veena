Assamese is the mother tongue of about 25 million people across Assam and Arunachal Pradesh, written in the Assamese script of the Eastern Nagari family. For a language with this many speakers, voice typing has usually meant uploading audio to a cloud service and hoping Assamese is supported at all. Veena takes a different route: **Assamese speech to text that runs entirely on your own device.** If you are new to on-device transcription, the [speech to text hub](/speech-to-text/) explains how Veena covers all 22 scheduled languages of India.

## Assamese speech to text with Veena

Veena's recognition engine is built on IndicConformer-600M-Multilingual from AI4Bharat, exported to ONNX and quantized so it can run entirely on-device. Veena's multilingual engine covers all 22 scheduled languages of India, and Assamese is transcribed with a dedicated vocabulary and decoding tuned for it. Tap the microphone, speak in Assamese, and within moments the transcript appears in an editable text box — with no internet connection required.

The on-device design matters more than it sounds. Transcription happens on your phone or computer rather than on a remote server, so your audio never leaves your device. You can dictate a confidential note, a patient summary, or a business idea without wondering where your words end up. Veena records 16 kHz mono audio and converts it locally with the on-device model.

## Why transcribe Assamese audio

Typing Assamese on a phone keyboard is slow: the script's vowel signs and conjunct forms mean many words take several keystrokes per syllable. Speaking is simply faster, which makes dictation useful in situations where you would otherwise switch to English to save time:

- Students in Assam and Arunachal Pradesh recording lecture notes and dictating assignments in Assamese.
- Journalists covering the state for newspapers and television, filing copy hands-free.
- Doctors and nurses writing patient notes and referrals in Assamese without touching a keyboard.
- Small business owners drafting letters, bills, and replies in Assamese.
- Content creators producing Assamese videos and podcasts, dictating scripts and captions.
- Families recording oral traditions — folk songs, storytelling, oral histories — and turning them into text for the next generation.

Dictation also helps accessibility: people who find keyboards difficult can still write in their own language. And every time someone produces text in Assamese instead of switching to English, the written language stays a little more alive.

## How to use Veena for Assamese

On your phone, install Veena from the app store and grant microphone permission — the only permission the app asks for. Open the app, tap the microphone, and speak; Veena supports both hold-to-record and tap-to-record modes, so you can dictate a short sentence or a long paragraph. The transcript appears in an editable text box where you can correct a word before copying or sharing it, and you can replay the last recording to check what you actually said.

On desktop, The Veena desktop app for Windows works differently but just as simply: a push-to-talk global shortcut lets you press a key in any application, speak Assamese, and have the text pasted directly into the app you are using — or copied to your clipboard. A local recording history keeps past dictations findable, and the model for your language downloads inside the app. If your work involves specialized vocabulary, the desktop app also supports custom models and optional post-processing such as filler-word removal and custom-word correction.

## Tips for better accuracy in Assamese

Speech recognition improves with a little practice, and these habits get you the cleanest Assamese transcripts:

- Speak in complete sentences rather than fragments. Recognition models do better when they can use context.
- Keep a steady pace. Rushing produces slurred syllables; pausing between sentences helps Veena separate them.
- Reduce background noise. Find a quiet spot or use a headset microphone, especially indoors.
- Avoid switching languages mid-sentence. Assamese speakers often mix in English or Bengali words; the more a single utterance stays in Assamese, the cleaner the transcript. If you need both languages, dictate a sentence in one, then a sentence in the other.
- Articulate word endings. Assamese speech often relaxes final vowels, so a slightly deliberate pronunciation helps the engine pick up the last syllable.
- Use the replay feature to check tricky passages, and edit the transcript — corrections never require retyping the whole document.
