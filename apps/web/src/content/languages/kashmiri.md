Kashmiri, spoken by about 7 million people, is the language of the Kashmir Valley and one of the oldest literary languages of the Indian subcontinent. It is written in the Perso-Arabic script, which is the official script used in education and administration, alongside a Devanagari tradition that appears in some publications and among communities outside the valley. For a language with such a rich heritage of poetry and storytelling, the tools for turning spoken words into written text have been surprisingly scarce. Veena changes that: **Kashmiri speech to text that runs entirely on your own device.**

## Kashmiri speech to text with Veena

Veena is a speech-to-text application for Indian languages, built on IndicConformer, a multilingual recognition model from AI4Bharat. The model runs on-device, and every language gets its own vocabulary and decoding configuration tuned for it. Kashmiri is one of them. Tap the microphone, speak in Kashmiri, and Veena turns your words into editable text instantly, with no internet connection and no account.

Veena's multilingual engine covers all 22 scheduled languages of India, including Kashmiri, Urdu, Dogri, Hindi, and Punjabi. Because transcription happens locally, your audio never leaves your device. That matters in journalism, education, and administrative work, where the content of a conversation is often sensitive.

## Why transcribe Kashmiri audio

Kashmiri is the language of everyday life in the valley, yet much official and written work in Jammu and Kashmir has historically happened in other languages. That is slowly changing, and voice transcription gives Kashmiri a practical place in writing.

Journalists and community media reporting in Kashmiri can dictate interviews and notes and edit them as text. Teachers can prepare lesson material in the language their students actually speak. Students can dictate essays and assignments instead of wrestling with keyboards that were never designed for Kashmiri. Families and cultural groups working to document oral traditions, folk songs, and local histories can capture them as text for archives. For the Kashmiri diaspora, dictating letters and messages in the mother tongue keeps a written connection to home.

Both script traditions matter here. The Perso-Arabic script is what most educated speakers read and write, while the Devanagari tradition keeps the language accessible to speakers who learned to read in that script. Whatever tradition you write in, the first step is the same: get the spoken language into text.

Kashmiri is also finding its way back into classrooms as a subject and a medium of study. Teachers who prepare lesson material in the language can now draft it by speaking, and learners can practice writing without getting stuck on keyboard layouts that were never designed for Kashmiri sounds.

## How to use Veena for Kashmiri

On your phone, open the Veena app, grant the microphone permission, and choose hold-to-record or tap-to-record mode. Speak in Kashmiri, and the transcript appears in an editable text box. Copy it, share it, or replay your last recording to check the result.

On a computer, The Veena desktop app for Windows offers push-to-talk with a global shortcut. Press the key, speak, and the transcribed text is pasted directly into the application you are using, or copied to your clipboard. A recording history with retention settings helps you manage your dictations, and the model for your language downloads inside the app. Optional post-processing such as filler-word removal keeps long dictations tidy.

## Tips for better accuracy in Kashmiri

Kashmiri has sounds that do not appear in most other North Indian languages, so clear speech matters. Speak at a natural pace and avoid rushing. Keep background noise low, because the recognizer listens for fine distinctions in vowels and consonants. Keep the microphone close and steady. If you mix Kashmiri with Hindi, Urdu, or English within one sentence, the engine may struggle; try to keep each sentence in one language. When a word comes out wrong, correct it in the editable transcript and move on. Short, focused recordings usually transcribe better than one long, meandering session.
