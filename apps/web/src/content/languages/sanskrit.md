Sanskrit (संस्कृतम्) is the classical language of India, with a literary and philosophical tradition spanning thousands of years. Today it is spoken by about 25,000 people as a living language, and a quiet revival is under way: Sanskrit-medium schools, study groups, and communities such as Mattur in Karnataka keep it in daily use, while scholars, teachers, and students across the country engage with it every day. For anyone working with Sanskrit, typing Devanagari can be the slowest part of the work. Veena is built differently: **Sanskrit speech to text that runs entirely on your own device, with no account and no cloud.**

## Sanskrit speech to text with Veena

Veena's recognition engine is built on IndicConformer, a multilingual model from AI4Bharat, exported to ONNX and quantized so it runs entirely on-device. Veena's multilingual engine covers all 22 scheduled languages of India — Sanskrit is one of them — and each language gets its own vocabulary and decoding tuned for it. Tap the microphone and speak in Sanskrit, and Veena records 16 kHz mono audio and transcribes it locally with the on-device model. The transcript appears in an editable text box, ready to copy, share, or refine. No internet connection is needed for transcription, and your audio never leaves your device.

Sanskrit deserves more than a borrowed vocabulary, and Veena treats it as its own language: Devanagari output, Sanskrit's distinctive word stock, and decoding tuned for the way Sanskrit is actually spoken — in classrooms, study circles, and revival communities. That makes Veena a practical companion for the growing number of people who want to speak and write Sanskrit rather than only read it.

## Why transcribe Sanskrit audio

Sanskrit's revival is driven by people speaking it, and speech to text supports that work directly:

- Students in Sanskrit-medium schools and universities can dictate practice passages, answers, and compositions in Sanskrit, then edit them in the transcript box.
- Teachers preparing lessons and study materials can speak their notes instead of typing Devanagari character by character.
- Study groups and conversation circles can record their sessions and turn spoken Sanskrit into written records for members to review.
- Scholars and researchers can capture dictated drafts of articles and translations without losing the flow of thought to slow typing.
- Learners practicing pronunciation can speak a passage, see it in Devanagari, and compare it with the written source.

Because Veena works fully offline, it is also useful in libraries, classrooms, and village study centers where connectivity is limited or not available. Your Sanskrit audio never has to leave your device to become text.

## How to use Veena for Sanskrit

On the mobile app, open Veena, tap the microphone, and speak in Sanskrit. The transcript appears in an editable text box — copy it, share it, or fix a word before sending. You can hold to record or tap to record, replay your last recording to check what you said, and edit transcripts after the fact. The app asks for only one permission: microphone access.

On the desktop app for Windows, use push-to-talk or a global shortcut to start dictating from any application. The audio is transcribed locally and the text is pasted directly into the app you are using, or copied to your clipboard. A local recording history helps you find past dictations, and the model for your language downloads inside the app. Optional post-processing such as filler-word removal and custom-word correction can be enabled; the transcription itself is fully on-device.

## Tips for better accuracy in Sanskrit

- Sanskrit joins words together through sandhi, and sentences run together when spoken quickly. A measured pace with a slight pause between words helps the transcript stay readable.
- Speak at a steady, natural rhythm — neither rushed nor artificially slow — and pause briefly between sentences.
- Keep the microphone close and background noise low; recitation in a noisy room produces a messier transcript than a quiet one.
- Pronounce the long and short vowels distinctly. In Sanskrit, length changes meaning, and clear vowel articulation helps the engine keep words apart.
- Names of texts, teachers, and places often need a small edit; the transcript box makes that quick.
- Use the replay feature to hear your last recording if a passage came out wrong.
- On desktop, enable custom-word correction for the names of texts and terms you use often.
